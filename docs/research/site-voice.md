# Make the site sound like football people

**Contents:** [The setup](#the-setup-a-short-voice-page-a-writer-pass-and-a-test-that-fails) · [What sticks](#instructions-alone-hold-most-of-the-time-not-every-time) · [Testing](#test-strings-in-ci-screens-with-a-judge-and-people-twice-a-season) · [Words](#words-uk-draft-products-say-requests-and-offers-not-claims-and-adds) · [FM-isms](#fm-isms-keep-the-desk-swap-the-game-mechanics) · [Conclusion](#conclusion)

The recommended setup is three layers, each doing one job. First, a short voice page (`docs/voice.md`, under 60 lines): three or four "this, not that" traits, a table of tone by moment (error, empty screen, deadline, win, loss) and five before-and-after pairs taken from our own `words.ts` strings. Second, a **matchday-voice agent** that runs as its own step after each build and before review, edits only words files, and rewrites the builder's text with fresh eyes. Third, the existing glossary test grows into a word lint that **fails the build only on rules with no false alarms** (dashes, banned stock phrases, Title Case labels, US words); everything subjective goes to the writer or reviewer as advice. This matters because Anthropic's own guidance says agent instructions are followed "most of the time, not every time", so anything that must always hold has to be a test ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). For testing, a cheap lint on every pull request catches wrong words; a judge model on a different family from the writer, shown a real UK line as a yardstick, catches wrong tone; and a blind "which sounds like a real football site?" form for league members twice a season is the only direct measure of "sounds like us". Skip AI detectors and reading-age scores: neither works on two-to-fifteen-word strings. On words, UK draft products say **waiver request, trade offer, free agency, drop, top/bottom of the table**; FPL slang about prices and shared ownership (template, differential, enabler) means nothing in a draft; and Football Manager's game-mechanic labels (morale ladders, "Fairly Good" ratings, match sharpness) read as a video game, not a pundit. The existing word list lives in `docs/research/lexicon.md`; this report doesn't repeat it.

## The setup: a short voice page, a writer pass and a test that fails

The best published style guides split a **short, fixed voice** from a **long list of checkable rules**. Mailchimp keeps four traits, each written as "X but not Y" ("smart but not snobbish"), and lets the tone shift with the reader's mood ([Mailchimp](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/)). Shopify's Polaris starts buttons with the verb ("Add apps") and writes errors as the fix ("Add a discount code", not "Discount can't be blank") ([Polaris](https://polaris.shopify.com/content/actionable-language), [errors](https://polaris.shopify.com/content/error-messages)). GOV.UK pairs every banned word with a replacement ([GOV.UK A to Z](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/)). Monzo, the nearest UK model for warm-but-plain, built its voice from a staff survey so people would actually use it, and its canonical rule is "We're sorry", never "We'd like to apologise" ([Monzo](https://monzo.com/tone-of-voice/)). A Monzo forum thread shows the risk on the other side: warmth can read as patronising ([Monzo community](https://community.monzo.com/t/monzos-tone-of-voice/120691?page=3)).

For us that means one cheap change to what exists: the glossary's Avoid column gains a **Use instead** column, so every blocked word comes with its fix. The voice page holds the traits, the tone table and the example pairs, nothing else. Anthropic says **three to five varied examples** steer tone more reliably than rules, and a rule with its reason beats a bare "never" ([Anthropic prompting](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices)).

The matchday-voice agent (`.claude/agents/matchday-voice.md`) gets Read, Edit and Grep only, may change only words files, loads the voice page, rewrites the changed strings and reruns the word test. It runs as a separate step because a fresh agent "isn't swayed by the reasoning that produced the code" ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)); builders write for function, and a writer with no attachment to their phrasing fixes it. The builders' prompt shrinks to one line: every string goes in `words.ts`, and the writer owns the wording. pr-reviewer gains one scoped line ("flag copy that breaks voice.md; quote it, suggest a fix"), because a reviewer told to find problems "will always find something" (same source). No study compares "builders told to write well" with a separate writer pass for UI copy, so this rests on vendor guidance, not measured results.

Last, a feedback loop: each time Craig rewrites a string, it becomes a before-and-after pair or a banned word. Prune when the pairs pass about ten.

## Instructions alone hold most of the time, not every time

What gets ignored is predictable. Long guides lose lines: in one benchmark the best models followed only **68% of instructions at 500**, favouring the ones near the start ([IFScale, arXiv 2507.11538](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11538)). Anthropic says that if a line keeps being missed despite emphasis, it "belongs in a hook" ([Claude Code best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). Practitioners report em dashes surviving explicit rules until a hook scanned written files and sent Claude back ([DEV](https://dev.to/gentic_news/stop-writing-more-rules-use-hooks-to-enforce-your-claudemd-1a02); anecdotal).

Noisy checks get ignored too. GitLab fails its build only on error-level rules, shows warnings in the review, and keeps suggestions to the editor; it warns against rules "too subjective" and asks "how often authors would reasonably ignore it" before adding one ([GitLab Vale docs](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/documentation/testing/vale)). The lesson: **promote a rule to failing only once it raises no false alarms on today's text** ([Fern](https://buildwithfern.com/post/automate-style-checks-vale)).

That shapes the word lint. Vale, the standard prose linter, reads comments in TypeScript, not string values ([Vale code formats](https://docs.vale.sh/formats/code)), so the cheapest path is growing the vitest glossary test, which already reads words files. A ready-made "AI tells" pack exists with **134 rules, all set to fail** ([Vale ai-tells](https://vale.sh/explorer/ai-tells)), but switching it on wholesale would flag real football verbs. Pick rules one at a time. Wikipedia's list of AI-writing signs is the best source for them: "serves as" for "is", "not just X, but also Y", trailing "-ing" phrases, inflated words like "pivotal" and "testament" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)).

| Check | Where | Fails the build? |
|---|---|---|
| Glossary Avoid words, US words | vitest test | Yes |
| Em and en dashes, "!" outside a short allowed list | vitest test | Yes |
| Banned stock phrases (seamless, unlock, elevate, dive in, delve, journey, "not just … but") | vitest test | Yes, once clean on today's text |
| Title Case labels and headings | vitest test | Yes (sentence case is GOV.UK's and Sky's) |
| "Please", "Oops", "successfully", "submitted" | vitest test | Start as warning |
| Length cap per string type (button, toast, heading) | vitest test | Yes |
| Rule of three, over-explaining, stiff tone | matchday-voice, then reviewer | No, judgement |

## Test strings in CI, screens with a judge, people twice a season

Each test method catches something different, and two catch nothing useful.

| Method | Catches | Misses | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Word lint on `words.ts` | wrong word, US spelling, stock phrases, overlong strings | tone, context | free, every pull request |
| Word counts from UK sites | words we use that real sites don't ("standings" where they say "table") | anything not on the pair list | a monthly script |
| Judge model, pairwise | stiff or robotic tone, game words, wrong register | Craig's taste until checked against it | small, changed strings only |
| Screenshot read | a label that only fails next to what it labels | wording rules | uses ui-verifier's shots |
| Blind league form, highlighter test | "doesn't sound like us", confusing text | statistical proof | twice a season |
| AI detectors | nothing useful at this length | everything | skip |
| Reading-age scores | nothing useful for microcopy | everything | skip |

**Word counts.** The standard way to spot words a text overuses against a reference is keyness, a log-likelihood score ([Rayson and Potts, Lancaster](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/157063/1/06_Rayson_Potts_18jun19_clean.pdf)); free tools like AntConc run it ([AntConc](https://groups.google.com/g/antconc/c/3A1tdBRuW6A)). General corpora such as Google Books' British set help only with spelling, because keyness depends heavily on the reference's genre ([Pojanapunya and Todd](https://kirim.kmutt.ac.th/converis/portal/detail/Publication/601394176?lang=en_GB)); books won't know "waivers". No football word-count set exists, so we'd build one: a script fetches pages from UK sites, keeps only `{word: count, site}` and throws the text away. Keep counts, not text: the UK's mining exception covers non-commercial research only ([Bristows](https://www.bristows.com/news/the-text-and-data-mining-copyright-exception-in-the-uk-for-the-sole-purpose-of-research-for-a-non-commercial-purpose-what-does-it-cover/)). That counts are safe is an inference, not legal advice, and each site's terms weren't checked. A curated pair list ("line-up/lineup", "table/standings") gives more signal than open keyness.

**Judge model.** Strong judges agree with people **over 80% of the time, as often as people agree with each other** ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)), but they have known biases. Swapping the order of two answers often flips the verdict ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926v1)); models rate their own writing higher ([Panickssery et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13076v1)); and they prefer longer answers, which works against short UI copy. So: compare two versions rather than score one, run both orders and count only agreements, give the reason before the verdict, show one real UK line of the same kind as a yardstick, tell it to prefer the shorter when meaning is equal, and use **a different model family from the writer**. Anthropic says to check a judge's reliability before scaling it ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/test-and-evaluate/develop-tests)): Craig or league members label 30 to 50 pairs once, and the judge stays only if it mostly agrees. No study has tested judges on sports microcopy.

**Screens, not strings.** A label read alone loses what it labels; copy should be judged "in the context of your design" ([UX studio](https://www.uxstudioteam.com/ux-blog/ux-copywriting)). The judge or reviewer reads ui-verifier's 1440 screenshots, not the bare file.

**People.** A blind form is the only direct test of "sounds like us". About **20 raters show a clear split; a 60/40 split needs nearer 100** ([Koji](https://www.koji.so/docs/preference-testing-guide), corrected by own check). One league of 10 to 16 can't prove much, so use many pairs rather than many raters: 20 sentence pairs, ours against a rewrite or against a real FPL or BBC line with names swapped, in random order, with a "can't tell" option. Many "can't tell" answers on ours-against-real is a pass. Add a highlighter test, where readers mark text useful, confusing, or (our third colour) "doesn't sound like a football site" ([dscout](https://dscout.com/people-nerds/content-testing)). A fill-the-gap test doubles as a word check: blank the football term ("Set your ___ before the deadline") and see what fans write ([18F](https://18f.gsa.gov/2016/04/19/looking-at-the-different-ways-to-test-content)).

**Skip detectors and reading scores.** Detector accuracy collapses under 50 words: one detector's hit rate fell from **0.72 to 0.32** at 50 words, and another flagged 61% of 50-word human texts as AI ([arXiv 2610.18151](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.18151)). Buttons are far shorter, and detectors answer "was a machine involved?", not "does a football person talk like this?". GOV.UK's designers say reading formulas "need at least 30 sentences" and measure readability, "not content quality" ([GOV.UK design notes](https://designnotes.blog.gov.uk/2022/11/21/what-do-robots-know-about-good-content/)). A length cap per string type does the useful part.

## Words: UK draft products say requests and offers, not claims and adds

The Premier League's own Draft guides and Draft Fantasy, the UK rival, share a plain set. Where they differ from US apps, it's mostly nouns: **UK products speak of requests and offers; US apps of claims and proposals.**

| Use | Avoid | Why |
|---|---|---|
| waiver request | waiver claim | PL: "You make a waiver request" ([PL Draft](https://www.premierleague.com/news/1245430)); Draft Fantasy's page has no "claim" ([DF](https://docs.draftfantasy.com/essentials/waivers.md)) |
| waiver order, waiver queue | waiver wire | DF: "waiver queue order is set to the reverse draft order" (same) |
| trade offer, accept, reject | trade proposal | PL: "A trade offer involves proposing to swap" ([PL trading](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/1245445)) |
| object (action), Vetoed (status) | veto as a manager's verb | PL: "If 50 per cent of managers object" (same) |
| Proposed, Accepted, Rejected, Withdrawn, Expired, Processed | longer status sentences | PL's trade statuses, one past-tense word each (same) |
| drop, release; sign, pick up | add, add/drop | DF: "the player you drop goes to waivers" ([DF](https://docs.draftfantasy.com/essentials/transfers.md)) |
| snake draft, pick, round, draft order | — | PL: "the pick order is reversed each round" ([PL Draft](https://www.premierleague.com/news/1245430)) |
| free agency | — | PL: "free agency mode will be active until the Gameweek deadline" (same) |
| top/bottom of the table | — | PL: "finished BOTTOM of the table" (same) |
| retained player | keeper (for a kept player) | in UK football "keeper" means goalkeeper |
| injured, ruled out | IR, injured reserve | no UK product has it |
| waiver bids, budget | FAAB on its own | DF labels it "Waiver Bids (FAAB)" ([DF](https://docs.draftfantasy.com/essentials/waivers.md)) |

Two of these touch our own words. The spec says **"claims"** for waivers; UK products say "request", so that is Craig's call. And the Not now list mentions **keeper leagues**; if that is ever built, it needs another name. The glossary's "shortlist" (with "watchlist" avoided) differs from PL Draft's "watchlist" but suits our FM-leaning desk; nothing to change.

**FPL slang splits cleanly.** Words about prices and shared ownership mean nothing in a draft, where each player has one owner: **avoid differential, template, effective ownership, enabler, premium, price rise, overall rank** ([PL glossary](https://www.premierleague.com/en/news/2683145)). Words about real football carry straight over: **nailed-on** (hyphened), rotation, minutes, flagged with a "75% chance of playing" ([Hub](https://www.fantasyfootballhub.co.uk/articles/unavailable-players-in-fantasy-premier-league)), eye test, set and forget, xG, punt, knee-jerk ([FFS](https://www.fantasyfootballscout.co.uk/2026/11/14/the-nailed-on-fpl-picks-for-the-busy-festive-period)). Double and blank gameweeks are fine, spelt out first time. FPL's chips (bench boost, triple captain, free hit) stay out unless a league builds one. "Most transferred in" becomes "most signed" or "most dropped".

**How UK media write the basics.** Sky Sports writes **"Saturday 21st November"** on fixtures and "Kick-off at 3:00pm", 12-hour with am/pm and kick-off hyphenated; "Manchester City vs Fulham"; table columns **Pl W D L F A GD Pts**; and sentence-case headlines with short names (Man Utd, Spurs) ([Sky fixtures](https://www.skysports.com/premier-league-fixtures), [Sky table](https://www.skysports.com/premier-league-table), [Sky news](https://www.skysports.com/premier-league-news)). GOV.UK agrees on "5:30pm" over "17:30" but drops the ordinal ("21 November") ([GOV.UK A to Z](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/)). Pick one form and fix it in the voice page: "Sat 21 Nov, 3pm" fits tight labels. Confirmation messages should say what happened in two or three words ("Line-up saved", "Offer sent"), never "Your request has been submitted successfully": GOV.UK's confirmation heading is just "Application complete" ([GOV.UK Design System](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/confirmation-pages/)), and it says there's "usually no need to say 'please'".

What isn't confirmed: BBC Sport refused every fetch, so its time format, "FT"/"HT" labels and short club names are unchecked. Match words such as equaliser, brace, booked, sent off, added time and "ruled out" are standard UK usage but no source was fetched for them this round. PL Draft's in-app button text needs a signed-in look.

## FM-isms: keep the desk, swap the game mechanics

The site deliberately borrows Football Manager's structure (Mail, scouts, reports, Tactics), and that stays. What reads oddly is FM's **game-mechanic vocabulary**, which ordinary fans never say. That includes the abbreviations CA, PA, ME, OI and PPM ([FM Scout](https://www.fmscout.com/a-football-manager-abbreviations-and-terminology.html)); morale from "Abysmal" to "Superb" ([Passion4FM](https://www.passion4fm.com/how-to-improve-players-morale-happiness-in-football-manager/)); and the squad-report ladder "Fairly Good, Decent, Useful" ([SI manual](https://community.sports-interactive.com/manual/football-manager-2021/your-squad-team-report-and-dynamics-r766), from a search snippet). It also covers "match sharpness" as a hidden stat ([RealSport101](https://realsport101.com/article/football-manager-202-conditioning-overhauled-ahead-of-release-match-sharpness-draining-fatigue)), "private conversation", Dynamics, Hierarchy and the 1-to-20 attribute scale. Say "in form", "fit", "rusty", "wants out" instead; those fan phrasings come from general knowledge, not a fetched source.

Tactical words split by whether they crossed into normal football talk. **False nine, inverted full-back, wing-back, holding midfielder and number 10 are fine**: FourFourTwo explains the inverted full-back as mainstream, used by almost half of PL clubs ([FourFourTwo](https://www.fourfourtwo.com/features/the-inverted-full-back-football-tactics-explained)), and the glossary already uses it as a trait. **Regista, trequartista, mezzala, enganche and raumdeuter read as hipster terms** ([Balls.ie](https://www.balls.ie/football/football-hipster-terms-football-manager-2015/141874)). The "Twitter tactico" layer (half-spaces, rest defence, field tilt, gamebreakers) gets openly mocked: Planet Football says using "gamebreakers" makes you "a pretentious berk" ([Planet Football](https://www.planetfootball.com/quick-reads/11-tactico-phrases-that-fry-our-brains-field-tilts-pausa-gamebreakers-more)). These belong on the writer's banned list, with plain swaps.

## Conclusion

The real shift is treating wording as its own job with its own owner. Builders will keep writing function-first text, and no amount of rules will fully stop that; a writer pass with a short voice page fixes the tone, and a failing test holds the few things that must never slip. Craig's own rewrites are the most valuable input of all, since each one becomes a test or an example the agents can't argue with.

Two calls are Craig's. Should "waiver claim" become "waiver request" to match both UK draft products? And should the word lint fail on stock AI phrases from the start, or warn for a fortnight while today's text is cleaned? Everything else here (the voice page, the writer agent, the lint list, the judge set-up) follows from the research and can be built once he says go.
